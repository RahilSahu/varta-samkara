Ancient Indian history is the highest-weightage static section in UPSC and UPPSC Prelims. Questions repeat from a fixed set of topics: Harappan sites and their specialities, Vedic literature and polity, Buddhist and Jain councils, Mauryan administration and Ashokan edicts, Gupta achievements, and the southern kingdoms. This page covers the full syllabus from prehistory to the Cholas in plain language, with timelines and one-liners at the end.

Prehistory: the Stone Ages

Indian prehistory is divided by the tools people used. In the Palaeolithic (Old Stone Age), humans used rough chipped stone tools and lived by hunting; the Sohan valley in Punjab is a famous site. The Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age) brought smaller tools called microliths; Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh, with its painted rock shelters, is the best-known site. The Neolithic (New Stone Age) brought farming, polished tools, and settled village life; Mehrgarh in present-day Pakistan, dating to around 7000 BCE, is one of the earliest farming settlements in the world. The Chalcolithic age added copper tools alongside stone. Remember the order: stone, then copper with stone, then bronze, then iron.

Harappan Civilization (c. 2600-1900 BCE)

The Harappan or Indus Valley Civilization was a Bronze Age urban civilization spread across present-day Pakistan and western India. Its mature phase runs roughly 2600 to 1900 BCE. The two largest cities were Mohenjo-daro (in Sindh, Pakistan) and Harappa (in Punjab, Pakistan). The civilization is known for town planning: grid-pattern streets, covered drains, and citadels built on raised platforms. There is no clear evidence of kings or palaces, which is itself a favourite exam point.

The Harappans traded with Mesopotamia (called Meluhha in Mesopotamian texts), used a uniform system of weights, and wrote in the still-undeciphered Indus script. Their decline is attributed to a mix of river shifts, climate change, and possibly invasions; no single cause is proven.

Vedic Period (c. 1500-600 BCE)

The early or Rigvedic period (c. 1500-1000 BCE) is known from the Rigveda, the oldest Veda, composed in the Sapta Sindhu region (land of seven rivers, centred on the Indus and Saraswati). Society was tribal and pastoral; the raja was a tribal chief, not a king, assisted by assemblies called the Sabha and Samiti, and the smaller Vidatha. There was no standing army and no taxation system yet.

The later Vedic period (c. 1000-600 BCE) shifted east to the Ganga-Yamuna doab. Iron tools and the plough made agriculture the base of the economy. Kingship became hereditary and rituals like the Rajasuya and Ashvamedha sacrifices grew grand. Society hardened into four varnas: Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra. The key texts:

VedaContent
RigvedaOldest; 1,028 hymns in 10 mandalas; Gayatri mantra in mandala 3
SamavedaBook of chants, set to music

The Brahmanas explain rituals, the Aranyakas are forest texts, and the Upanishads (Vedanta, "end of the Vedas") deal with philosophy; the principle "Satyameva Jayate" comes from the Mundaka Upanishad. The six Vedangas (limbs of the Vedas) include grammar (Panini's Ashtadhyayi) and astronomy.

Mahajanapadas and the Rise of Magadha

By the 6th century BCE, northern India had 16 Mahajanapadas (great states), listed in the Buddhist text Anguttara Nikaya. The important ones for exams: Magadha (capital Rajgir, later Pataliputra), Kosala (Shravasti), Vatsa (Kaushambi), Avanti (Ujjain), Kashi, and the republican Vrijji/Lichchhavi confederacy (Vaishali). This was also the age of second urbanisation and the use of punch-marked coins.

Magadha rose above the rest because of its iron deposits (weapons), elephants (army), fertile land, and ambitious rulers. Its dynasties in order: Haryanka (Bimbisara, who founded Rajgir and was a contemporary of the Buddha; Ajatashatru, who fortified Pataliputra), Shishunaga (shifted capital to Vaishali), and Nanda (Mahapadma Nanda, called the first empire-builder of Magadha; the last Nanda, Dhanananda, was overthrown by Chandragupta Maurya with Chanakya's guidance).

Buddhism and Jainism

Both religions arose in the 6th century BCE as reactions against Vedic ritualism and caste rigidity.

Gautama Buddha (563-483 BCE), born at Lumbini to the Shakya chief, left home at 29 (the Great Renunciation), attained enlightenment at Bodh Gaya under the Bodhi tree at 35, gave his first sermon at Sarnath (the Dhammachakkappavattana, setting the wheel of dhamma in motion), and died at Kushinagar (Mahaparinirvana). His teachings: the Four Noble Truths (suffering exists, it has a cause, it can end, the Eightfold Path ends it) and the Middle Path between luxury and asceticism. The Tripitaka is the Buddhist canon: Vinaya (monastic rules), Sutta (teachings), Abhidhamma (philosophy).

Mahavira (540-468 BCE), the 24th Jain Tirthankara, was born at Kundagrama near Vaishali. Jainism teaches the Triratna (three jewels): right faith, right knowledge, right conduct; and the five vows, including ahimsa (non-violence) and aparigraha (non-possession). After Mahavira, Jainism split into Digambaras (sky-clad) and Shvetambaras (white-clad).

Mauryan Empire (c. 321-185 BCE)

Chandragupta Maurya founded the empire around 321 BCE with his mentor Chanakya (Kautilya), author of the Arthashastra, the great text on statecraft. Bindusara succeeded him. Then came Ashoka (reigned c. 268-232 BCE), the most famous Mauryan. His invasion of Kalinga in 261 BCE caused such slaughter that he converted to Buddhism and adopted the policy of Dhamma (moral rule). He sent missions abroad, including his son Mahendra and daughter Sanghamitra to Sri Lanka.

Ashoka's edicts, inscribed on rocks and pillars in Prakrit using Brahmi script, are the earliest deciphered Indian inscriptions (deciphered by James Prinsep in 1837). The Sarnath Lion Capital of his pillar is India's national emblem. Mauryan administration was highly centralised: the empire was divided into provinces headed by kumara (princes), with a vast spy network described in the Arthashastra. The last Mauryan, Brihadratha, was killed by his general Pushyamitra Shunga around 185 BCE.

Post-Mauryan Kingdoms

After the Mauryas, north-west India saw Indo-Greek kings (Menander, called Milinda, converted to Buddhism; his dialogue with Nagasena is the Milindapanha), Shakas, and Parthians. The Kushanas under Kanishka (c. 78 CE, founder of the Shaka era) controlled the Silk Road trade; the Fourth Buddhist Council was held in his reign, and Gandhara art (Greco-Roman style Buddhist sculpture) flourished. In the Deccan, the Satavahanas (capital Pratishthana/Paithan) revived trade and issued lead coins.

Gupta Empire (c. 320-550 CE): the Golden Age

The Guptas are called India's golden age for their achievements in science, art and literature. Chandragupta I (c. 319-335 CE) founded the era; Samudragupta (c. 335-375 CE), praised in the Allahabad pillar inscription by Harisena, conquered widely and is called the "Napoleon of India"; Chandragupta II Vikramaditya (c. 375-415 CE) saw the empire at its height, with the Chinese traveller Fa-Hien visiting India. His court had the Navratnas (nine gems), including Kalidasa (Shakuntala, Meghaduta) and the astronomer Varahamihira.

Aryabhata (5th century CE) explained that the earth rotates on its axis and calculated pi; Brahmagupta later formalised zero. In art, the Ajanta cave paintings and the Sarnath Buddha belong to this age. Administration was decentralised compared to the Mauryas, with strong village bodies. Land grants to Brahmins (agrahara) began in this period. Later Guptas declined under Hun invasions; Skandagupta repelled the Huns around 455 CE.

Harsha and the Southern Kingdoms

Harshavardhana of Kannauj (reigned 606-647 CE) was the last great Hindu emperor of the north. Banabhatta's Harshacharita is his biography; the Chinese pilgrim Hiuen Tsang visited his court and studied at Nalanda, the great Buddhist university. Harsha was defeated by the Chalukya king Pulakeshin II at the Narmada, a famous exam fact.

In the south: the Pallavas of Kanchi built the shore temples and rock-cut rathas of Mahabalipuram (Mamallapuram); the Chalukyas of Badami built the temples at Aihole, Badami and Pattadakal; the Rashtrakutas carved the Kailasa temple at Ellora from a single rock and patronised Kannada literature. The imperial Cholas (850-1279) under Rajaraja I (985-1014 CE) and Rajendra I (1014-1044 CE) built the Brihadeeswara temple at Thanjavur, sent a naval expedition to Srivijaya in 1025, and ran a remarkable system of village self-government recorded in the Uttaramerur inscription (c. 920 CE), which details committee-based local administration by lot.
