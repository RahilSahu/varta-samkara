Capitalism, socialism, communism, mixed economy. These words get thrown around in every political debate, but they are often used loosely. Here is a plain breakdown of what each system actually means, and what countries really follow.

Capitalism is built on private ownership. Individuals and companies own businesses and property, and the profit motive drives production. Prices are largely set by supply and demand in a free market, with the state playing a limited role.

Socialism shifts ownership and control toward the state or the community. The stated goal is equality: key industries and resources are managed collectively, and wealth is redistributed so that basic needs are met for everyone.

## From communism to the mixed economy

Communism goes further than socialism, calling for the abolition of private property altogether and a classless society. A command economy is its practical cousin: the state plans what is produced, in what quantity, and at what price, rather than leaving those decisions to markets.

Neoliberalism, by contrast, pushes in the opposite direction: deregulation, privatisation and open markets, with minimal state interference in the economy.

Most countries do not run on any pure model. A mixed economy blends private enterprise with state intervention, and a welfare state adds strong public services like healthcare and education on top of a market economy.

## What countries actually follow

India is a classic mixed economy: private companies operate freely, but the state runs major banks, railways and welfare schemes. The Nordic countries pair capitalist markets with expansive welfare states. China runs a market-driven economy under tight state control. The United States leans capitalist with selective regulation.

The reality check: no major economy today is purely capitalist or purely socialist. Every system is a mix, and the real debate is about where to draw the line between the market and the state.
