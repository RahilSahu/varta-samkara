Francis Halzen wins 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for IceCube neutrino work

Belgian-American particle physicist Francis Halzen has won the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for his "decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin", the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced on October 6.

Halzen, 82, is affiliated with the University of Wisconsin-Madison in the United States, which serves as the lead institution for the IceCube Neutrino Observatory, a cubic-kilometre neutrino detector buried deep in Antarctic ice at the South Pole. His research into capturing neutrinos there dates back to the 1980s, and under his leadership an international team of researchers and engineers built the instrument that opened a new window on the cosmos.

## A new kind of astronomy

Committee chair Mark Pearce praised Halzen's "tenacity and scientific vision", saying they "have paved the way for a new kind of astronomy". Neutrinos are near-massless particles that travel across the universe almost undisturbed, and IceCube's detection of high-energy neutrinos from astrophysical sources lets scientists trace them back to violent cosmic events that light-based telescopes cannot see.

The Physics award is the second Nobel of the 2026 season, following the Medicine Prize announced on October 5. The Chemistry Prize is next, due on October 7. The prize money totals 12 million Swedish kronor (about $1.2 million), and medals will be presented by Sweden's King Carl XVI Gustaf at a ceremony in Stockholm on December 10.
