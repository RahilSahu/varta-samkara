The Indian History Our Schools Skipped

R
Rahil Sahu
Founder, Varta & Samkara
On this page

  • Kingdoms that outlived empires
  • The invaders' own record
  • Who held the pen

Open a school history textbook and count the pages. The invaders get chapters. The kings who stood against them get paragraphs. Some get a line. Many get nothing at all.

This is the other half of the story: the Hindu kings who won battle after battle, the kingdoms that stood for centuries while empires rose and fell around them, and the parts of the Mughal record our textbooks chose not to print.

The kings who beat the invaders

**Prithviraj Chauhan** is remembered for losing the Second Battle of Tarain in 1192. He is rarely remembered for winning the First Battle of Tarain in 1191, fought near Taraori in present-day Haryana, where Muhammad Ghori himself was wounded and forced to retreat to Ghazni. The defeat made the textbooks. The victory did not.

**Rana Sanga of Mewar** is traditionally credited with 18 victories against the sultans of Delhi, Malwa and Gujarat, and his record earns the number. At Khatoli in 1517 he crushed Ibrahim Lodi of Delhi and lost an arm to his wounds. The next year at Dholpur he beat Lodi again. In 1519 at Gagron he defeated Mahmud Khilji II, the Sultan of Malwa, took him prisoner, and then set him free. In 1527 his Rajput forces drove Babur's detachments out of Bayana fort. Only at Khanwa, near Agra in March 1527, did Babur's artillery break his confederacy. The man who humiliated sultans for a decade is remembered for a single defeat.

**Maharana Pratap** never bowed to Akbar, and Akbar never managed to subdue him. At Haldighati on 18 June 1576, Pratap rode out against a far larger Mughal army under Man Singh and fought his way out alive with his kingdom intact. In 1582 at Dewair, his forces stormed the Mughal garrison and took back territory. He died in 1597 still free, still undefeated in spirit, having spent twenty-five years denying the most powerful emperor in India the one submission he wanted most.

**Hemu, Hemchandra Vikramaditya,** won 22 engagements between 1553 and 1556, first against Afghan rebels and then against the Mughals. On 7 October 1556 he captured Delhi itself at Tughlaqabad and was crowned Vikramaditya at Purana Qila. For a month, a Hindu king sat on Delhi's throne. It ended at the Second Battle of Panipat on 5 November 1556, when an arrow struck him in the eye and he was beheaded on Bairam Khan's orders. Akbar's empire was built on the elimination of a rival our books never name.

**Chhatrapati Shivaji** needs no exaggeration. On 10 November 1659 at Pratapgad he destroyed the Bijapur general Afzal Khan and his army. On 4 February 1670 his commander Tanaji Malusare recaptured Sinhagad from the Mughals at the cost of his life, and Shivaji renamed the fort for him. On 17 October 1670, returning from the sack of Surat, his army beat the Mughal commander Daud Khan in open battle at Vani-Dindori. In 1674 he was crowned Chhatrapati at Raigad, founding a Hindu sovereignty the Mughals would spend the next century failing to extinguish.

**Sambhaji Maharaj,** crowned in 1680, held Aurangzeb's entire Deccan invasion at bay for nearly nine years. The full military weight of the Mughal Empire could not finish him in open war, so it took him by treachery: on 1 February 1689 his small detachment was surprised at Sangameshwar. After weeks of torture for refusing Aurangzeb's terms, he was executed on 11 March 1689 at Tulapur. Aurangzeb expected Maratha resistance to die with him. It only grew fiercer.

**Peshwa Bajirao I** never lost a battle he commanded. At Palkhed on 28 February 1728 his light cavalry encircled the Nizam-ul-Mulk and forced a treaty recognizing Maratha rights across the Deccan, a masterpiece of manoeuvre warfare still studied today. At Bhopal on 24 December 1737 he shattered a combined Mughal army sent by the emperor himself, and Malwa passed to the Marathas. His younger brother **Chimaji Appa** matched him: in 1739 Chimaji's siege broke the Portuguese at Vasai, ending their dominance north of Goa. Two brothers, and between them the Mughals, the Nizam and the Portuguese all learned the same lesson.

Kingdoms that outlived empires

India's Hindu kingdoms did not merely resist. They endured, for spans of time that make most empires look brief.

The **Cholas** carry their name across some fifteen centuries, from the Sangam age to 1279. At their height under Rajaraja I and Rajendra I, their fleets crossed the Bay of Bengal and struck Srivijaya in 1025, and their temples at Thanjavur and Gangaikonda Cholapuram still stand as proof of what they built. The **Pandyas** ruled the far south for well over a thousand years. The **Ahoms** held Assam from 1228 to 1826, six hundred years, and no Mughal emperor ever took their kingdom. In 1671 their commander Lachit Borphukan destroyed a Mughal fleet on the Brahmaputra at Saraighat, and in 1682 they expelled the Mughals from Guwahati for good.

**Vijayanagara** rose in 1336 as a Hindu bulwark in the Deccan and stood for three centuries, reaching its zenith under Krishnadevaraya (1509-1529), when its capital Hampi was among the largest cities on earth. The **Gurjara-Pratiharas** held northern India for three centuries and, under Nagabhata I and Mihira Bhoja, stopped the Arab advance into India cold in the eighth and ninth centuries. The **Chalukyas** of Badami, the **Rashtrakutas**, and the **Palas** of Bengal each ruled for two to four centuries. The **Guptas** gave India its classical golden age, and Skandagupta turned back the Hun invasion in the fifth century. In Kashmir, the **Karkota** king Lalitaditya built an empire in the eighth century that Kalhana's chronicle stretches from Punjab to Bengal.

**Mewar** never truly fell. Its Guhila and Sisodia kings held Chittorgarh across a thousand years of sieges, and its story is written in the names of Bappa Rawal, Rana Kumbha, Rana Sanga and Maharana Pratap. The **Marathas**, from Shivaji's coronation in 1674, carried Hindu sovereignty from Attock to Cuttack until 1818. **Travancore** under Marthanda Varma did what no Asian power had done before: in 1741 at Colachel it destroyed a Dutch expeditionary force, among the earliest defeats of a European colonial power in Asia. And **Ranjit Singh's Sikh Empire** (1799-1849) made Lahore the seat of a kingdom the British themselves chose not to challenge in his lifetime.

These were not footnotes. They were the main story of the subcontinent for most of its history.

The invaders' own record

A complete history records both sides. Aurangzeb had his brother Dara Shikoh, the designated heir, executed in August 1659. He deposed his father Shah Jahan and kept him confined in Agra Fort from 1658 until his death in 1666. His eldest daughter Zeb-un-Nissa, a noted Persian poet, spent the last twenty years of her life locked in Salimgarh Fort for corresponding with her rebel brother.

The celebrated romance of the Taj has its own facts. Mumtaz Mahal died on 17 June 1631 at Burhanpur giving birth to her fourteenth child. Akbar's court, meanwhile, maintained a harem household that Abu'l Fazl numbered at some 5,000 women, with around three dozen wives taken in political alliances. These are not slanders. They are the records of their own chroniclers.

And the empire itself: at its peak under Aurangzeb it covered some 3.2 million square kilometers and ruled about 150 million people, the largest polity India had seen. But it was never the whole of India. The Ahoms of Assam were never absorbed. The Kerala coast, Travancore and Cochin, stayed free. Portuguese Goa was never Mughal land. Maratha resistance was never crushed, even by 1707. Most of the subcontinent, yes. All of it, no.

Who held the pen

For the first thirty years after Independence, India's education portfolio was held by nine ministers: Maulana Abul Kalam Azad (1947-1958), K.L. Shrimali (1958-1963), Humayun Kabir (1963), M.C. Chagla (1963-1966), Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed (1966-1967), Triguna Sen (1967-1969), V.K.R.V. Rao (1969-1971), Siddhartha Shankar Ray (1971-1972), and S. Nurul Hasan (1972-1977).

Azad himself was a scholar of Arabic and Persian, a journalist from his teens, twice Congress president, the founder of the Sahitya Akademi and the University Grants Commission, and a force behind the IITs. The question is not his learning. The question is what generations of schoolchildren were given to read, and what was left out, while these men held the pen. The kings in this article are the answer to the second half of that question.

Why it matters

None of this asks anyone to hate the past. It asks for proportion. Prithviraj's victory at Tarain, Sanga's eighteen battles, Pratap's defiance, Hemu's march on Delhi, Shivaji's coronation, Sambhaji's nine-year stand, Bajirao's undefeated campaigns, the Chola fleets, the Ahom navy at Saraighat, Marthanda Varma's triumph at Colachel: this is not a footnote to someone else's story. It is the story of India, and it deserves its chapters.
