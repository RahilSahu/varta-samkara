The Constitution of India is the supreme law of the land. It lays down the framework of the country's political system, the powers of its institutions, and the rights and duties of its citizens. It was adopted by the Constituent Assembly on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950. The Drafting Committee was chaired by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar, and Dr. Rajendra Prasad presided over the Constituent Assembly.

## The Preamble

"WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens: JUSTICE, social, economic and political; LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation; IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION."

Sovereign means India is free to conduct its own internal and external affairs. Socialist and Secular were added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976: the first points to a welfare state committed to reducing inequality, the second to equal respect for all religions by the state. Democratic means the government is elected by the people, and Republic means the head of state is elected rather than hereditary. The Preamble promises Justice (social, economic and political), Liberty (of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship), Equality (of status and of opportunity), and Fraternity (assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation; the word Integrity was also added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976).

## Salient Features

The Constitution of India is the lengthiest written constitution in the world. It began with 395 Articles in 22 Parts and 8 Schedules, and it has grown through amendments since.

It draws from many sources. The Government of India Act, 1935 contributed the federal scheme, the judiciary, and emergency provisions. From Britain came the parliamentary system and the rule of law; from the United States, Fundamental Rights, judicial review, the impeachment of the President, and the independence of the judiciary; from Ireland, the Directive Principles of State Policy; from Canada, a strong centre with residuary powers vested in the Union; and from Australia, the Concurrent List and provisions on freedom of trade and commerce.

It is federal in form but unitary in spirit. Powers are divided between the Union and the States, the Constitution is written and rigid in parts, and the judiciary is independent. At the same time, single citizenship, a strong centre, emergency provisions, and All-India Services give it a unitary bias.

India follows the parliamentary system. The President is the nominal head of state, while real executive power rests with the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers, who are collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha.

The judiciary is independent and integrated. There is a single hierarchy of courts from the Supreme Court at the apex down to the district courts, and all courts follow the law declared by the Supreme Court.

Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles sit side by side. The rights are justiciable and enforceable in courts, while the Directive Principles are non-justiciable guidelines that direct the state toward a welfare society.

India has single citizenship: every Indian is a citizen of India only, with no separate state citizenship. Elections are held on the basis of universal adult franchise, so every adult citizen has the right to vote.

## Parts of the Constitution

Part I (Articles 1 to 4): The Union and its Territory. It covers the name and territory of India, the admission of new states, and Parliament's power to reorganise states and alter their boundaries.

Part II (Articles 5 to 11): Citizenship. It lays down who was a citizen at the commencement of the Constitution and gives Parliament the power to make laws on citizenship.

Part III (Articles 12 to 35): Fundamental Rights. It guarantees the six fundamental rights to citizens and provides remedies for their enforcement.

Part IV (Articles 36 to 51): Directive Principles of State Policy. These are guidelines for the state in making laws and policies, and they are not enforceable in any court.

Part IVA (Article 51A): Fundamental Duties. Added in 1976, it lists the duties every citizen owes to the nation.

Part V (Articles 52 to 151): The Union. It covers the President, the Vice-President, the Council of Ministers, Parliament, the Supreme Court, and the Comptroller and Auditor-General.

Part VI (Articles 152 to 237): The States. It covers the Governor, the state executive, state legislatures, High Courts, and subordinate courts.

Part VII: This Part dealt with Part B states of the First Schedule. It was repealed by the 7th Amendment in 1956.

Part VIII (Articles 239 to 242): The Union Territories. It provides for their administration by the President.

Part IX (Articles 243 to 243O): The Panchayats. Added by the 73rd Amendment in 1992, it gives constitutional status to rural local self-government.

Part IXA (Articles 243P to 243ZG): The Municipalities. Added by the 74th Amendment in 1992, it gives constitutional status to urban local self-government.

Part IXB (Articles 243ZH to 243ZT): The Co-operative Societies. Added by the 97th Amendment in 2011.

Part X (Articles 244 and 244A): The Scheduled and Tribal Areas. It provides for their special administration.

Part XI (Articles 245 to 263): Relations between the Union and the States. It covers the distribution of legislative and administrative powers.

Part XII (Articles 264 to 300A): Finance, Property, Contracts and Suits. It covers taxation, borrowing, property rights, and government contracts.

Part XIII (Articles 301 to 307): Trade, Commerce and Intercourse within the territory of India.

Part XIV (Articles 308 to 323): Services under the Union and the States, including the Public Service Commissions.

Part XIVA (Articles 323A and 323B): Tribunals. Added in 1976 to provide for administrative and other tribunals.

Part XV (Articles 324 to 329A): Elections. It establishes the Election Commission and bars courts from interfering in electoral matters in certain ways.

Part XVI (Articles 330 to 342): Special Provisions relating to certain classes, including reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and provisions for Other Backward Classes and Anglo-Indians.

Part XVII (Articles 343 to 351): Official Language. It declares Hindi in Devanagari script as the official language of the Union.

Part XVIII (Articles 352 to 360): Emergency Provisions. It covers national, state, and financial emergencies.

Part XIX (Articles 361 to 367): Miscellaneous provisions, including protection for the President and Governors.

Part XX (Article 368): Amendment of the Constitution. It lays down the procedure for amending the Constitution.

Part XXI (Articles 369 to 392): Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions.

Part XXII (Articles 393 to 395): Short Title, Commencement, Authoritative Text in Hindi, and Repeals.

## The Twelve Schedules

The First Schedule lists the names of the states and union territories and describes their territories.

The Second Schedule lays down the salaries, allowances, and privileges of the President, Governors, judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, the Comptroller and Auditor-General, and others.

The Third Schedule gives the forms of oaths and affirmations for ministers, judges, MPs, MLAs, and other constitutional functionaries.

The Fourth Schedule allocates seats in the Rajya Sabha to the states and union territories.

The Fifth Schedule deals with the administration of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes.

The Sixth Schedule deals with the administration of tribal areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram.

The Seventh Schedule divides subjects into the Union List, the State List, and the Concurrent List.

The Eighth Schedule lists the official languages recognised by the Constitution. There are 22 of them.

The Ninth Schedule validates certain land reform and other laws against judicial review. It was added in 1951.

The Tenth Schedule lays down disqualification of MPs and MLAs on the ground of defection. It was added by the 52nd Amendment in 1985.

The Eleventh Schedule lists 29 subjects of Panchayats, such as agriculture, rural housing, and drinking water. It was added by the 73rd Amendment in 1992.

The Twelfth Schedule lists 18 subjects of Municipalities, such as urban planning, water supply, and public health. It was added by the 74th Amendment in 1992.

## Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties

Right to Equality (Articles 14 to 18) guarantees equality before the law, prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth, provides equality of opportunity in public employment, abolishes untouchability, and abolishes titles.

Right to Freedom (Articles 19 to 22) protects six freedoms under Article 19, including speech and expression, and provides safeguards against arbitrary conviction, arrest, and detention, along with the protection of life and personal liberty under Article 21.

Right against Exploitation (Articles 23 and 24) prohibits traffic in human beings and forced labour, and prohibits the employment of children below 14 years in factories, mines, or hazardous work.

Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25 to 28) guarantees freedom of conscience and the free profession, practice, and propagation of religion, the right to manage religious affairs, freedom from taxes for religious purposes, and freedom from religious instruction in state-funded institutions.

Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29 and 30) protect the right of minorities to conserve their language, script, and culture, and their right to establish and administer educational institutions.

Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32) allows any person to move the Supreme Court directly for the enforcement of fundamental rights. Dr. Ambedkar called it the heart and soul of the Constitution.

Directive Principles of State Policy (Articles 36 to 51) are guidelines for the Union and State governments in making laws. They are not enforceable in any court, but they are fundamental in the governance of the country and aim to build a just, welfare-oriented society.

Fundamental Duties (Article 51A) were added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976. There were originally ten duties; an eleventh duty, requiring parents to provide educational opportunities to children between 6 and 14 years, was added by the 86th Amendment in 2002.

## Amendment Procedure (Article 368)

The Constitution can be amended in three ways. Some provisions can be changed by a simple majority of members present and voting, and these amendments fall outside Article 368. Most provisions need a special majority: a majority of the total membership of each House plus two-thirds of the members present and voting. Provisions affecting the federal structure need a special majority plus ratification by the legislatures of at least half of the states.

## Landmark Amendments

The 42nd Amendment (1976), often called the Mini-Constitution, added the words Socialist, Secular, and Integrity to the Preamble and introduced the Fundamental Duties.

The 44th Amendment (1978) reversed several changes of the 42nd Amendment. It removed the right to property from the list of fundamental rights, making it a legal right under Article 300A, and replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion" as a ground for proclaiming a national emergency.

The 52nd Amendment (1985) added the Tenth Schedule, which disqualifies MPs and MLAs who defect from their parties.

The 61st Amendment (1989) lowered the voting age from 21 years to 18 years.

The 73rd Amendment (1992) gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj institutions through Part IX and the Eleventh Schedule.

The 74th Amendment (1992) gave constitutional status to Municipalities through Part IXA and the Twelfth Schedule.

The 86th Amendment (2002) made free and compulsory education a fundamental right for children aged 6 to 14 through Article 21A.

The 101st Amendment (2016) introduced the Goods and Services Tax (GST), creating a unified indirect tax system across India.

The 103rd Amendment (2019) provided 10 percent reservation for Economically Weaker Sections in education and public employment.

The 106th Amendment (2023), the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, reserves one-third of seats in the Lok Sabha and state legislative assemblies for women.

## How to Study This for Exams

Learn the Parts, Schedules, and landmark amendments as tables and revise them often, since most questions come from these areas. Connect each Article to a current event or a landmark case to remember it longer. Practise previous years' question papers from UPSC, SSC, and state PCS exams to see which Articles and Amendments are asked most often.

This is a study summary for exam preparation; for legal purposes, always consult the official text of the Constitution of India.
