Economics is one of the highest-scoring sections in government exams because most questions repeat from a fixed set of concepts. This page covers the full syllabus for UPSC, SSC, Banking, and MPPSC: market fundamentals, national income, inflation and unemployment, money and banking, the budget and fiscal policy, and the Indian economy since 1951. Every term is defined in plain language first, because exam questions test understanding, not memorised full forms.

## Fundamentals of Economics

Economics is the study of how people and societies choose to use scarce resources. The starting point of all economics is scarcity: human wants are unlimited, but resources like land, time, and money are limited. Because of scarcity, every choice involves a trade-off. Adam Smith, who wrote The Wealth of Nations in 1776, is called the father of modern economics.

Economics has two branches. Microeconomics studies individual units, a consumer, a firm, or one industry: why onion prices rise or how a shop sets prices. Macroeconomics studies the whole economy: national income, employment, inflation, and growth. John Maynard Keynes founded modern macroeconomics during the Great Depression of the 1930s.

A need is something required for survival, like food and shelter. A want is a desire beyond needs, like a smartphone. Goods are tangible things you can touch, while services are intangible activities, like a haircut or a bank loan. Useful exam classifications: free goods like air cost nothing, while economic goods are scarce and priced; consumer goods satisfy wants directly (bread), while capital goods produce other goods (a tractor); public goods like street lights are provided by the government because nobody can be excluded from using them.

Demand is the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price. The law of demand says that, other things being equal, a higher price means lower quantity demanded, so demand curves slope downward. Rare exceptions: Giffen goods are inferior goods where a price rise makes poor buyers buy more of them, and Veblen goods are prestige goods where a higher price itself attracts buyers.

Only the good's own price causes movement along the demand curve. A shift of the whole curve happens when something else changes: consumer income, tastes, prices of related goods, or expectations. For example, rising incomes shift the demand curve for cars outward even at unchanged car prices.

Supply is the quantity producers are willing to sell at a given price. The law of supply says a higher price brings a larger quantity supplied, so supply curves slope upward. Only the good's own price moves along the curve; input costs, technology, or taxes shift it. The price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied is the equilibrium price.

Elasticity measures responsiveness in one line: the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. If demand changes more than price (elastic, value above 1), as with luxury goods, a small price cut boosts sales a lot. If demand barely changes (inelastic, value below 1), as with salt or medicines, price changes hardly affect quantity bought.

Markets are classified by how much competition exists:

| Market type | Sellers | Product | Price control | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect competition | Very many | Identical | None, price taker | Wheat mandi |
| Monopoly | One | Unique, no close substitute | Full | Railways in India |
| Oligopoly | A few | Similar or differentiated | Interdependent | Telecom, airlines |
| Monopolistic competition | Many | Differentiated by brand | Some | Restaurants, soaps |

Production needs four factors of production. Land means all natural resources. Labour is human effort, physical and mental. Capital means man-made tools used in production, like machines and buildings; money counts as capital only when it buys productive assets. The entrepreneur combines the other three, takes risks, and innovates; Joseph Schumpeter stressed innovation as the entrepreneur's key role.

Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative you give up when you make a choice. If you spend an hour studying economics, the opportunity cost is whatever else you could have done with that hour. Exams love this definition because it captures the whole idea of trade-offs in one line.

## National Income

National income is the total value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a year. The word final matters: only finished products are counted, not the raw materials used to make them, which would double-count the same value.

The most-used measure is Gross Domestic Product (GDP): the value of all final goods and services produced within the country's borders in a year. The standard formula is GDP = C + I + G + (X - M), where C is private consumption, I is investment, G is government spending, and (X - M) is net exports.

Related measures adjust GDP step by step. Gross National Product (GNP) = GDP + net factor income from abroad (income Indians earn abroad minus income foreigners earn in India). Net National Product (NNP) = GNP - depreciation, the wear and tear of machines and buildings. NNP at factor cost is called National Income. Also note: GDP at market price = GDP at factor cost + indirect taxes - subsidies.

Nominal GDP values output at current-year prices, so it rises when prices rise even if nothing more is produced. Real GDP values output at base-year prices, so it measures actual growth. The GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) x 100; it is a broad measure of the price level. Per capita income = National Income / population, and it is the simplest indicator of average living standards.

In India, national income is estimated by the National Statistical Office (NSO), which works under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation. The NSO was formed in 2019 by merging the Central Statistics Office (CSO) and the National Sample Survey Office (NSSO). The current base year for these estimates is 2011-12, meaning real GDP is measured in 2011-12 prices. A base-year revision is periodically done so the numbers reflect the modern economy.

The economy is divided into three sectors. The primary sector extracts from nature: agriculture, mining, fishing, and forestry. The secondary sector transforms raw materials: manufacturing, construction, and electricity. The tertiary sector provides services: banking, transport, education, health, IT, and tourism. In India the tertiary sector contributes the largest share of GDP (roughly half or more), while the primary sector still employs the most workers, a mismatch behind much of India's disguised unemployment.

## Inflation and Unemployment

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, which means the purchasing power of money falls. A little inflation is normal, but fast inflation hurts.

India measures inflation with two main indices. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) tracks retail prices paid by households and is computed by the NSO with base year 2012; it covers both goods and services and is the index RBI targets. The Wholesale Price Index (WPI) tracks wholesale prices of goods only and is computed by the Office of the Economic Adviser under DPIIT with base year 2011-12. Headline inflation is the overall index number; core inflation strips out volatile food and fuel prices to show the underlying trend.

The types of inflation, each in one line: demand-pull inflation happens when too much money chases too few goods; cost-push inflation happens when production costs like oil or wages rise and firms pass them on; creeping inflation is mild, below about 3 percent a year; walking inflation is moderate, roughly 3 to 10 percent; galloping or running inflation is fast, in double digits; hyperinflation is out of control, above 50 percent a month, as defined by economist Phillip Cagan; stagflation is the painful mix of stagnant growth with high inflation; deflation is a general fall in prices, which sounds pleasant but discourages spending and deepens slowdowns; disinflation means the inflation rate is falling but still positive; reflation is deliberate government action to push the economy back up out of deflation; skewflation means some goods inflate sharply while others see prices fall.

Causes include excess demand, deficit financing, rising input costs, higher oil prices, and bad monsoons cutting food supply. Effects are uneven: debtors gain by repaying in cheaper money while creditors lose; fixed-income earners and savers suffer; exports get costlier for foreign buyers.

Unemployment means people who are willing and able to work cannot find jobs. Its types: disguised unemployment is when more workers are engaged than needed, so extra workers add nothing, classic in Indian family farms; seasonal unemployment hits farm labourers and tourism workers in off-seasons; structural unemployment comes from a mismatch between workers' skills and available jobs; cyclical unemployment rises and falls with recessions and booms; frictional unemployment is the short gap when people switch jobs; educated unemployment means graduates cannot find work matching their qualifications.

In India, unemployment is measured by the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS), conducted by the NSO since 2017. It reports three measures: usual status (activity over the past year), current weekly status (past week), and current daily status (each day of the week).

## Money, Banking and RBI

Money has four functions: medium of exchange (no barter needed), measure of value (prices can be compared), store of value (purchasing power can be saved), and standard of deferred payment (loans and contracts are possible).

The Reserve Bank of India is the central bank. It was established on 1 April 1935 under the RBI Act, 1934, and was nationalised in 1949. Its roles: monopoly of issuing currency notes of Rs 2 and above (the Re 1 note and all coins are issued by the Government of India); banker to the government and the bankers' bank; controller of credit; custodian of foreign exchange reserves under FEMA, 1999; regulator of banks under the Banking Regulation Act, 1949; and lender of last resort in a crisis.

Through monetary policy the RBI manages the money supply and interest rates. Its tools, explained simply: the repo rate is the rate at which the RBI lends short-term money to banks against government securities, and raising it makes loans costlier, cooling inflation; the reverse repo rate is the rate at which the RBI borrows from banks, parking surplus funds with itself; the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) is the share of deposits banks must keep as cash with the RBI, earning no interest; the Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) is the share of deposits banks must keep in safe liquid assets like gold and government securities; the bank rate is the long-term rate at which the RBI lends without collateral, used as a penalty rate; Open Market Operations (OMO) mean the RBI buys government securities to inject money or sells them to absorb money; and the Marginal Standing Facility (MSF, 2011) lets banks borrow overnight against their SLR securities in emergencies. Cheap money policy (low rates) boosts growth; dear money policy (high rates) fights inflation.

Since 2016 India follows flexible inflation targeting on the recommendation of the Urjit Patel Committee: the target is 4 percent CPI inflation with a band of plus or minus 2 percent. Rates are set by the six-member Monetary Policy Committee: three RBI officials including the Governor as chair, and three government nominees.

India's banks, each in one line: commercial banks (SBI, HDFC Bank) accept deposits and lend, and are classed as public sector, private, or foreign; Regional Rural Banks (1975) serve rural areas and are owned 50 percent by the Centre, 35 percent by a sponsor bank, and 15 percent by the state government; cooperative banks (urban and rural) serve members on cooperative principles, with NABARD supervising the rural tier; Small Finance Banks (licensed from 2015) focus on small borrowers and priority-sector lending; Payments Banks (2015) can accept deposits but cannot lend; NABARD (1982) refinances agriculture and rural development and drove the SHG-bank linkage programme; SIDBI (1990) finances micro, small, and medium enterprises.

A Non-Performing Asset (NPA) is a loan on which interest or instalment has stayed unpaid for 90 days or more. NPAs are classed as substandard, doubtful, and loss assets as they age. The SARFAESI Act, 2002 lets banks seize collateral without going to court, and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 created a time-bound process to resolve failed companies. Financial inclusion means bringing everyone into banking; its flagship is the Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY, 2014), which opened zero-balance accounts with RuPay cards for the unbanked.

## Fiscal Policy and Budget

Fiscal policy is the government's use of its revenue and spending to influence the economy: taxing less and spending more boosts demand, while the reverse cools it. The Union Budget is the annual statement of this policy.

Government receipts come in two kinds. Revenue receipts neither create a liability nor reduce assets: tax revenue (income tax, GST, customs) and non-tax revenue (dividends from PSUs, fees, fines). Capital receipts either create a liability or reduce assets: borrowings, loan recoveries, and disinvestment proceeds. Expenditure is likewise split: revenue expenditure is day-to-day spending like salaries, interest payments, and subsidies, while capital expenditure creates assets like roads, railways, and machinery.

Three deficits are exam favourites. Fiscal deficit = total expenditure minus total receipts excluding borrowings; it shows how much the government must borrow. Revenue deficit = revenue expenditure minus revenue receipts; borrowing even for daily expenses, which is unhealthy. Primary deficit = fiscal deficit minus interest payments; the current year's borrowing excluding past debt burden.

The FRBM Act, 2003 (in force from 2004) commits the government to deficit discipline, targeting a fiscal deficit of 3 percent of GDP. The N. K. Singh Committee (2017) recommended a debt target of 60 percent of GDP (40 Centre, 20 states).

Direct taxes fall on income and are paid by the person levied, like income tax and corporate tax (administered by the CBDT). Indirect taxes fall on goods and services and are passed on to consumers, like GST and customs duty (administered by the CBIC).

The Goods and Services Tax (GST), launched 1 July 2017 via the 101st Amendment, replaced a maze of central and state taxes. It is a dual GST: on intra-state sales, CGST goes to the Centre and SGST to the state; on inter-state sales, IGST goes to the Centre. Three articles matter: 246A (both Centre and states can tax goods and services), 269A (IGST), and 279A (creates the GST Council, chaired by the Union Finance Minister, which sets rates and rules). Slabs were 5, 12, 18, and 28 percent plus a cess on luxury and sin goods; in 2025 the Council rationalised these mainly to 5 and 18 percent, with 40 percent on sin goods. States were compensated for revenue loss for five years.

The Union Budget is the Annual Financial Statement required by Article 112 of the Constitution. Since 2017 it is presented on 1 February, and the Railway Budget was merged into it the same year on the Bibek Debroy Committee's recommendation. Spending is either voted by Parliament or charged on the Consolidated Fund (like the President's salary), which is not voted.

## Indian Economy Overview

Independent India chose planned development. The Planning Commission was set up in 1950, and the First Five-Year Plan ran from 1951 to 1956, based on the Harrod-Domar model with focus on agriculture. The Second Plan (1956-61) followed P. C. Mahalanobis's model and stressed heavy industry. The Third Plan (1961-66) aimed at self-reliance but was hit by wars and drought, followed by a plan holiday of three annual plans (1966-69). The Fourth Plan (1969-74) sought growth with stability; the Fifth (1974-79) pushed poverty removal under the Garibi Hatao slogan; the Sixth (1980-85) and Seventh (1985-90) continued mixed-economy growth. The Eighth Plan (1992-97) absorbed the 1991 reforms, and the Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh (themed inclusive growth) followed. The Twelfth Plan (2012-17), themed faster, sustainable, and more inclusive growth, was the last; planning ended in 2017.

On 1 January 2015 the NITI Aayog replaced the Planning Commission. Where the Commission imposed top-down plans, NITI Aayog works on cooperative federalism: the Centre and states plan together, and it acts as a think tank rather than a fund allocator.

The 1991 LPG reforms were the turning point. A balance-of-payments crisis left forex reserves barely enough for three weeks of imports, forcing an IMF loan. Under P. V. Narasimha Rao and Manmohan Singh, India liberalised (rupee devalued, industrial licensing largely abolished), privatised (public sector's reserved areas shrank, disinvestment began), and globalised (foreign investment welcomed, trade opened). The MRTP Act was later replaced by the Competition Act, 2002.

Sector trends since then: services now contribute roughly half or more of GDP, industry about a quarter, and agriculture under a fifth, yet agriculture still employs the largest share of workers. Two revolutions shaped farming: the Green Revolution (from 1966, high-yielding seeds, associated with M. S. Swaminathan) made India self-sufficient in wheat; the White Revolution (Operation Flood, led by Verghese Kurien) made India the world's largest milk producer.

Poverty is estimated from consumption-expenditure surveys. The Tendulkar Committee (2009) set poverty lines including health and education spending, not just calories. The Rangarajan Committee (2014) set separate, higher rural and urban lines.

Two schemes appear constantly in exams. MGNREGA (2005 Act, rolled out 2006) guarantees 100 days of wage employment per year to every rural household. PM-KISAN (launched February 2019) gives Rs 6,000 per year in three instalments to landholding farmer families as income support.
