This page is an exam-oriented Geography summary for UPSC, SSC, Banking, and MPPSC aspirants, covering physical geography (earth, landforms, climate, oceans) and Indian geography (physiography, rivers, soils, agriculture, minerals). Every term is defined in simple language first; the focus is on facts exams actually ask: orders, heights, origins, leading states, and one-line differences.

## The Universe and the Earth

The solar system has the Sun at the centre with eight planets revolving around it in this order: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. Remember the order with the first letters: My Very Energetic Mother Just Served Us Noodles.

One feature per planet: Mercury, closest to the Sun, has extreme temperature swings. Venus is hottest (thick carbon dioxide air), spins backwards, and shines as the Morning and Evening Star. Earth alone has life, its surface about 71 percent water. Mars is red from iron oxide and holds Olympus Mons, the solar system's tallest volcano. Jupiter is largest; its Great Red Spot is a storm wider than Earth. Saturn has bright ice-and-rock rings. Uranus rolls around the Sun tilted on its side. Neptune is farthest with the fastest winds. Pluto became a dwarf planet in 2006.

Earth spins on its axis (rotation, about 24 hours), causing day and night, and travels around the Sun (revolution, about 365 and one-quarter days); with the 23.5-degree axial tilt, revolution causes the seasons, and the extra quarter-day gives a leap day every four years.

Location is read on an imaginary grid. Latitudes are horizontal circles parallel to the Equator (0 degrees): the Tropic of Cancer (23.5 degrees North), the Tropic of Capricorn (23.5 degrees South), the Arctic Circle (66.5 degrees North), and the Antarctic Circle (66.5 degrees South). Longitudes are vertical semicircles measured east or west of the Prime Meridian (0 degrees) at Greenwich, London.

Longitude decides time: Earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours, so 15 degrees of longitude equals one hour, and 1 degree equals 4 minutes. India uses 82.5 degrees East, passing near Mirzapur in Uttar Pradesh, as its Standard Meridian. Indian Standard Time (IST) is therefore GMT plus 5 hours 30 minutes, followed by the whole country.

A solar eclipse is the Moon coming between the Sun and Earth (new moon day); a lunar eclipse is Earth coming between the Sun and Moon (full moon day).

Tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level, caused mainly by the Moon's gravity, partly the Sun's. Spring tides (new and full moon days) occur when the Sun, Moon, and Earth line up and pulls combine, giving the highest high tides. Neap tides (quarter-moon days) occur when the Sun and Moon pull at right angles and partly cancel, giving the lowest high tides.

## Interior of the Earth and Landforms

Earth's three layers: the crust, a thin solid skin (oceanic 5 to 10 km, continental 30 to 70 km); the mantle, reaching about 2,900 km depth, whose soft upper asthenosphere lets plates drift; and the core, with a liquid iron-nickel outer core (its motion creates the magnetic field) and a solid iron-nickel inner core (kept solid by crushing pressure). Radius: about 6,371 km.

Rocks are classified by formation. Igneous rocks form when magma cools and hardens: granite (slow cooling underground) and basalt (fast cooling on the surface, as in the Deccan Traps). Sedimentary rocks form from layers of sand, mud, and organic matter piling up over time: sandstone and limestone; coal and petroleum occur in sedimentary rocks. Metamorphic rocks form when older rocks are transformed by heat and pressure: marble (from limestone), slate (from shale), gneiss (from granite).

Plate tectonics: Earth's rigid outer shell is cracked into plates drifting over the asthenosphere. Colliding plates raise mountains (the Himalayas, as the Indian plate crashed into the Eurasian plate); separating plates create new crust (Mid-Atlantic Ridge); plates sliding past each other trigger earthquakes (California's San Andreas Fault).

An earthquake is ground shaking from sudden energy release along a fault (a crustal crack). The focus (hypocentre) is the underground release point; the epicentre is the surface point above it. The Richter scale measures magnitude, total energy released (each whole step means about 32 times more energy). The Mercalli scale (I to XII) measures intensity, the damage seen at a place. Waves: P-waves, fastest, pass through solids and liquids; S-waves, slower, pass only through solids; surface waves, slowest but most destructive.

A volcano is a crustal opening erupting lava, ash, and gases. By activity: active volcanoes erupt regularly (Mount Etna, Italy; Barren Island in the Andamans, India's only active volcano); dormant volcanoes sleep but may wake (Mount Fuji, Japan); extinct volcanoes should not erupt again (Mount Kilimanjaro, Africa). Shapes: shield (broad, runny lava; Mauna Loa, Hawaii), composite or strato (steep layered cones; Mount Fuji), cinder cones (small steep ash piles).

Mountains: fold (squeezed rock layers; Himalayas, Alps, Andes, Rockies); block (crustal blocks lifted or dropped along faults; the Vosges, and India's Satpura, an uplifted block called a horst); volcanic (Mount Fuji); residual, the worn stumps of ancient ranges (the Aravalli, among the world's oldest). Plateaus are flat-topped highlands: the Deccan (ancient lava flows), Chotanagpur (mineral-rich), and the Tibetan Plateau, the world's highest, called the Roof of the World. Plains are flat lowlands: alluvial plains from river deposits (the Indo-Gangetic plain) and loess plains from wind-blown silt (China).

## Atmosphere and Climate

Five layers from the ground up: troposphere (to about 12 km), holding all weather with temperature falling with height; stratosphere, holding the ozone layer that absorbs ultraviolet rays; mesosphere, the coldest layer, where meteors burn; thermosphere, holding the ionosphere that reflects radio waves and producing auroras; exosphere, the thin outer fringe.

Insolation is incoming solar radiation. The heat budget tracks it: of 100 units reaching Earth, about 35 are reflected by clouds, ice, and deserts (reflectivity is called albedo), about 51 are absorbed by the surface, and about 14 by the atmosphere.

Hot air rises at the Equator (equatorial low, the calm doldrums), sinks near 30 degrees (subtropical high, the dry horse latitudes) and at the poles (polar high), with a subpolar low near 60 degrees. Winds blow from high to low pressure, bent by the Coriolis force: right in the Northern Hemisphere, left in the Southern. The three planetary winds: trade winds, from the subtropical highs to the Equator (the north-east trades, named for serving sailing ships); westerlies, between 30 and 60 degrees toward the subpolar lows, watering western coasts; polar easterlies, from the polar high to the subpolar low. Jet streams are narrow bands of very fast upper-troposphere winds blowing west to east that steer weather systems and flight times.

A cyclone is a low-pressure system with winds spiralling inward. Tropical cyclones form over warm oceans (above about 27 degrees C) with a calm centre called the eye; they are named hurricanes (Atlantic/Caribbean), typhoons (north-west Pacific), cyclones (Indian Ocean), or willy-willies (Australia). Temperate (extratropical) cyclones form in mid-latitudes where warm and cold air masses meet along fronts; their Indian form, the western disturbance, brings winter rain to the north-west. North Indian Ocean cyclones are named by the India Meteorological Department from rotating regional lists.

The Indian monsoon is a seasonal wind reversal: land heats and cools faster than the sea. In summer the heated landmass, especially the Tibetan Plateau, becomes low pressure and pulls in moist ocean air: the south-west monsoon, reaching Kerala around 1 June via the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal branches. In winter the cooled land turns high pressure and blows dry air seaward: the north-east monsoon, raining mainly on Tamil Nadu's Coromandel coast. Mawsynram (Meghalaya) gets the world's heaviest rainfall as the Khasi hills trap the Bay branch.

Koppen's climate types for India, in brief: Amw (monsoon, short dry winter; west coast south of Goa), As (monsoon, dry summer; Coromandel coast), Aw (tropical savanna; most of the peninsula), BShw (semi-arid steppe; north-west India), BWhw (hot desert; Thar, western Rajasthan), Cwg (subtropical, dry winter; Gangetic plain), Dfc (cold humid winters; north-eastern highlands), E/ET (tundra and polar; high Himalayas, Ladakh).

El Nino is unusual warming of the eastern Pacific off Peru; it weakens the monsoon and usually means below-normal Indian rainfall. La Nina is unusual cooling of the same waters; it strengthens the monsoon and usually means above-normal rainfall.

## Oceans

Ocean-floor relief: continental shelf, the gently sloping submerged edge of a continent (rich in fish and oil); continental slope, dropping steeply to the abyssal plain, the vast flat deep floor; mid-ocean ridge, a submarine chain where new crust forms; trench, a deep narrow depression (the Mariana Trench, about 11,000 m at Challenger Deep, is Earth's deepest point); seamounts, submerged volcanic peaks, and guyots, their flat-topped eroded remains.

Ocean currents are sea-bound rivers driven by winds, temperature, and salinity. Warm currents move from the Equator poleward; cold currents move from the poles equatorward.

| Current | Type | Coast affected |
|---|---|---|
| Gulf Stream | Warm | Eastern USA |
| North Atlantic Drift | Warm | Western Europe (keeps it mild) |
| Kuroshio | Warm | Eastern Japan |
| Brazil Current | Warm | Eastern Brazil |
| Labrador Current | Cold | North-eastern Canada (meets the Gulf Stream; great fishing grounds) |
| Canary Current | Cold | North-western Africa |
| Peru (Humboldt) Current | Cold | Western South America (nutrient upwelling) |
| Benguela Current | Cold | South-western Africa |

The Somali Current off the Horn of Africa is unique: it reverses direction with the monsoon seasons.

Coral reefs are built by tiny polyps in warm, shallow, clear tropical water: fringing reefs hug the shore; barrier reefs lie farther out behind a lagoon (Australia's Great Barrier Reef is the largest); atolls are rings around a submerged island (Lakshadweep). Rising seas cause coral bleaching, the whitening and death of corals.

Straits and channels near India: the Palk Strait between Tamil Nadu and Sri Lanka separates Palk Bay from the Gulf of Mannar; the Gulf of Mannar, between south-eastern Tamil Nadu and Sri Lanka, is a marine biosphere reserve; the 8 Degree Channel separates the Maldives from Minicoy (Lakshadweep); the 9 Degree Channel separates Kavaratti from Minicoy; the 10 Degree Channel separates the Andamans from the Nicobars.

## Indian Physiography

India's landforms fall into five divisions.

1. The Northern Mountains: the Himalayas plus the north-eastern hills (Patkai, Naga, Mizo, Garo, Khasi, Jaintia). Three parallel Himalayan ranges: the Greater Himalayas (Himadri), innermost and highest at about 6,000 m average with the great peaks and glaciers; the Lesser Himalayas (Himachal), 3,700 to 4,500 m, with hill stations Shimla, Mussoorie, and Nainital; the Shiwaliks, the 900 to 1,200 m foothills. Flat valleys between the Lesser Himalayas and Shiwaliks are called duns (Dehradun).

2. The Northern Plains: the vast alluvial plain of the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra. From north to south: bhabar (rocky foothills where streams sink underground), terai (marshy belt where they re-emerge), bhangar (older, higher alluvium), khadar (new fertile floodplain alluvium renewed yearly).

3. The Peninsular Plateau: the ancient stable Deccan tableland. The Western Ghats (Sahyadri) form its continuous, higher western edge near the coast; the Eastern Ghats form its broken, lower eastern edge; they meet at the Nilgiri Hills. The Palghat Gap is the main pass through the Western Ghats.

4. The Coastal Plains: the Western Coastal Plain is narrow with estuaries, split into Konkan (Mumbai to Goa), Kannad, and Malabar (Kerala). The Eastern Coastal Plain is wide and fertile with big deltas, split into the Coromandel coast (Tamil Nadu) and Northern Circars (Andhra Pradesh, Odisha).

5. The Islands: the Andaman and Nicobar Islands (Bay of Bengal) are volcanic, hosting India's only active volcano on Barren Island; Lakshadweep (Arabian Sea) is coral atolls.

Highest peaks: K2 or Godwin Austen (8,611 m, Karakoram, Pakistan-occupied Kashmir) is India's highest peak as claimed. Kanchenjunga (8,586 m, Sikkim, on the Nepal border) is the highest Himalayan peak in India. Nanda Devi (7,816 m, Uttarakhand) is the highest peak located entirely within India. In the south, Anamudi (2,695 m, Kerala) tops the Western Ghats and peninsular India; Mahendragiri (1,501 m, Odisha) tops the Eastern Ghats.

Key passes, one line each: Nathu La (Sikkim) links India with Tibet/China on the old Silk Route. Shipki La (Himachal Pradesh) is where the Sutlej enters India from Tibet. Bomdi La (Bomdila, Arunachal Pradesh) links the state with Tibet. Khardung La (Ladakh) is among the world's highest motorable passes, linking Leh with the Nubra valley.

## Rivers of India

Himalayan rivers (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra) versus peninsular rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Narmada, and others):

| Feature | Himalayan rivers | Peninsular rivers |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Glaciers and snowfields | Monsoon rainfall, mostly the Western Ghats |
| Flow | Perennial, fed by snowmelt all year | Seasonal, dependent on monsoon rain |
| Course | Very long, carve deep gorges through mountains | Shorter, flow over the plateau in shallow valleys |
| Valleys | Antecedent (older than the Himalayas, cut through them), deep V-shaped | Old graded landscape, shallow valleys, waterfalls at plateau edges |
| Use | Irrigation and navigation in the plains | Little navigation; ideal dam sites at falls for hydropower |

The Indus rises from the Bokhar Chu glacier near Lake Mansarovar in Tibet, enters India at Leh in Ladakh, and drains through Pakistan into the Arabian Sea. Its tributaries, the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej, give Punjab its name, "land of five rivers". The 1960 Indus Waters Treaty governs sharing with Pakistan.

The Ganga (about 2,525 km, India's longest river) starts as the Bhagirathi from the Gangotri glacier (Gomukh) in Uttarakhand and takes the name Ganga at Devprayag, where it meets the Alaknanda. Left-bank tributaries: Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi (the Sorrow of Bihar for its floods), Mahananda. Right-bank: Yamuna, Son, Damodar. The Yamuna rises at Yamunotri and receives the Chambal, Betwa, and Ken. The Ganga reaches the Bay of Bengal through the Sundarbans, the world's largest delta, shared with the Brahmaputra.

The Brahmaputra rises as the Yarlung Tsangpo from the Chemayungdung glacier in Tibet, enters Arunachal Pradesh as the Siang/Dihang, crosses Assam as the Brahmaputra, and enters Bangladesh as the Jamuna. Tributaries: Dibang, Lohit, Subansiri, Teesta. Majuli in Assam is among the world's largest river islands.

The Godavari (about 1,465 km, longest peninsular river) rises at Trimbakeshwar near Nashik in Maharashtra and is called the Dakshin Ganga. Tributaries: Penganga, Wainganga, Manjra, Indravati. It drains into the Bay of Bengal.

The Krishna rises at Mahabaleshwar in Maharashtra, receives the Bhima, Tungabhadra, and Musi, and drains into the Bay of Bengal. The Narmada rises at Amarkantak in Madhya Pradesh and flows west through a rift valley between the Vindhya and Satpura ranges into the Arabian Sea: India's longest west-flowing river. The Tapi rises at Multai in Madhya Pradesh and also flows west to the Arabian Sea. The Mahanadi rises at Sihawa in Chhattisgarh and builds a large delta in Odisha. The Kaveri rises at Talakaveri in Karnataka's Brahmagiri hills, receives the Hemavati, Kabini, and Bhavani, and drains into the Bay of Bengal.

## Soils, Vegetation and Agriculture

Major soils, with formation, crops, and regions:

- Alluvial: river-deposited in the Northern Plains and deltas; most fertile and widespread; khadar is new floodplain alluvium, bhangar is older higher alluvium. Crops: rice, wheat, sugarcane, pulses. Regions: Punjab, Haryana, UP, Bihar, West Bengal.
- Black (regur, black cotton soil): from weathered Deccan basalt lava; clayey, moisture-retentive, cracks deeply in summer (self-ploughing). Best crop: cotton. Regions: Maharashtra, western MP, Gujarat, parts of Andhra Pradesh.
- Red: from weathered crystalline rock; red from iron oxide; needs fertiliser. Crops: wheat, potato, pulses, millets. Regions: Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, eastern Maharashtra, Odisha.
- Laterite: from intense leaching in heavy-rainfall areas; hard and brick-like when dry. Crops: cashew, tea, coffee, rubber. Regions: Western Ghats, Kerala, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu hills.
- Desert (arid): sandy, saline, poor in organic matter; crop: bajra with irrigation (western Rajasthan).
- Mountain (forest): thin and acidic on Himalayan slopes. Uses: orchards, tea, spices.

Vegetation follows rainfall. Tropical evergreen forests (over 200 cm rain; Western Ghats, north-east, Andamans) stay green year-round (rosewood, ebony). Tropical deciduous (monsoon) forests, the most widespread, shed leaves in the dry season: moist (100 to 200 cm; teak, sal) and dry (70 to 100 cm). Thorn and scrub forests (under 70 cm; Rajasthan, interior Deccan) carry acacia and cactus-like plants. Himalayan forests change with altitude: deciduous to coniferous (pine, deodar) to alpine meadows. Mangroves grow in tidal deltas, chiefly the Sundarbans, named for the Sundari tree.

Three cropping seasons. Kharif: sown with the south-west monsoon in June-July, harvested September-October (rice, maize, jowar, bajra, cotton, groundnut, soybean). Rabi: sown October-November, harvested March-April (wheat, barley, gram, mustard, peas). Zaid: short summer crops, March to June (watermelon, cucumber, fodder).

The Green Revolution (mid-1960s) used high-yielding variety (HYV) seeds, fertilisers, and irrigation, starting with wheat in Punjab, Haryana, and western UP. M. S. Swaminathan is its father in India; Norman Borlaug, breeder of Mexican dwarf wheat, is the father of the global Green Revolution.

Key agro terms: horticulture (fruits, vegetables, flowers), sericulture (silkworms for silk), apiculture (bees for honey), pisciculture (fish farming), shifting cultivation (jhum in the north-east, podu in Odisha/AP; slash-and-burn of temporary plots), plantation agriculture (large single-crop tea, coffee, rubber farms), contour ploughing (ploughing along slope curves to check erosion).

## Minerals and Energy

Major minerals and leading states: iron ore, Odisha (Keonjhar, Mayurbhanj), plus Karnataka, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand. Coal: Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Odisha (mostly Gondwana type: high ash, low sulphur). Bauxite (aluminium ore): Odisha (Koraput, Kalahandi). Manganese: Madhya Pradesh, then Maharashtra, Odisha. Mica: Andhra Pradesh (Nellore). Copper: Madhya Pradesh (Malanjkhand) and Rajasthan (Khetri). Gold: Karnataka (Kolar, Hutti). Limestone: Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan. Petroleum: Assam (Digboi, India's oldest refinery), Gujarat, offshore Mumbai High.

Conventional energy: coal, petroleum, natural gas, hydropower, nuclear. Non-conventional (renewable): solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biomass. Key facts: coal is India's biggest source of commercial energy and electricity. Tarapur (Maharashtra) was the first nuclear station (1969); others: Rawatbhata, Kalpakkam, Narora, Kaiga, Kakrapar. Kerala's monazite sands hold large thorium reserves, the base of the three-stage nuclear programme. Bhadla Solar Park (Rajasthan) is among the world's largest. Tamil Nadu leads in wind power. Top tidal potential: Gulfs of Kutch and Khambhat. Geothermal: Manikaran (HP), Puga valley (Ladakh).
