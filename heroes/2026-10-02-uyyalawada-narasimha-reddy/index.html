Uyyalawada Narasimha Reddy: The Polygar Who Rose a Decade Before 1857

Ten years before the revolt of 1857, a dispossessed chieftain in the Ceded Districts of the Madras Presidency raised an army of five thousand villagers and held off the East India Company for months. His name was Majjera Narasimha Reddy, remembered as Uyyalawada Narasimha Reddy, and the British hanged him in 1847 and kept his skeleton in a cage for thirty years as a warning.

Reddy was born around 1806, the youngest of three brothers in a Polygar family of Uyyalawada in Koilkuntla taluk, now in Andhra Pradesh's Nandyal district. The Polygars were hereditary chieftains who administered villages and collected revenue; under Company rule their jaghir grants were abolished or shrunk to nominal sums, and their inam lands were encroached upon. Stripped of the privileges his family had held for generations, Reddy turned to armed resistance.

The revolt began in mid-1846. On 10 July his men attacked the Koilkuntla treasury, killing the tahsildar and seizing the funds. When the Company sent troops, Reddy defeated them at Giddalur on 23 July. For months he ran a guerrilla campaign across the Nallamala hills, raiding treasuries and officials, while the British shuffled collectors and offered large rewards for his capture. Unable to catch him in the field, they imprisoned his family at Kadapa to draw him out. Betrayed by an informant, he was captured at midnight on 6 October 1846 near Ramabhadrunipalle. He was chained, paraded blood-stained through the streets of Koilkuntla so that none would dare revolt, and tried by a special commissioner. Some 900 of his associates were charged; 112 were convicted and sentenced to prison terms of five to fourteen years, and some were exiled to the Andamans.

Reddy himself was convicted of waging war against the government, along with murder and dacoity, and sentenced to death. On 22 February 1847 he was publicly hanged at Koilkuntla. To terrorise the countryside, the British kept his skeleton hanging in a cage at the Koilakuntla fort until 1877, when the scaffold decayed and was not repaired.

Historians are careful about what the revolt was. As historian K. Venugopal Reddy has argued, it was a revolt against colonial oppression rooted in the destruction of the Polygar order, not an expression of modern nationalism; the 2019 Telugu biopic Sye Raa, starring Chiranjeevi, dressed it in the language of nationalism. Both things can be true: the revolt was not the 1857 uprising, but it was the first mass armed rebellion in Andhra against the British, and it kept alive a memory of resistance that later freedom fighters could point to.

That memory lived mostly in Rayalaseema's villages and folk songs. Reddy left no writings and no organization behind him. He is remembered because the British made a public spectacle of his death, and then forgot him for the same reason: he belonged to a local rebellion that the national narrative, written later, had little use for.
