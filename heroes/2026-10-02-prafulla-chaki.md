Prafulla Chandra Chaki, also known by the alias Dinesh Chandra Roy, was born on 10 December 1888 in Bogra district of the Bengal Presidency, now in Bangladesh. Drawn to the revolutionary movement around 1906, he joined the Jugantar group in Calcutta, where he trained in physical culture and in the making of bombs. He was 19 when his leaders gave him the mission that would end his life.

The target was Douglas Kingsford, the Chief Presidency Magistrate of Calcutta, notorious among nationalists for the harsh sentences he handed down to Indians, including young men tried for sedition. Alarmed for his safety, the government had transferred Kingsford to Muzaffarpur in Bihar as District Judge in March 1908. Chaki and a fellow teenager, Khudiram Bose, were dispatched to assassinate him there.

On the evening of 30 April 1908, the two lay in wait outside the European Club in Muzaffarpur and hurled a bomb at a carriage they believed carried Kingsford. The carriage was not his. It carried Mrs. and Miss Kennedy, the wife and daughter of a barrister, Pringle Kennedy, who were both killed. Kingsford survived. The pair fled into the night, and a manhunt swept Bihar.

Khudiram was arrested the next morning at Waini railway station. Chaki made it to Mokama railway station, where the police closed in on him. Rather than submit to arrest and the interrogation that would follow, he shot himself in the head. He died on 1 or 2 May 1908 (sources differ on the day), aged 19, one of the youngest martyrs of the early revolutionary wave.

The bombing convulsed Bengal politics. Mahatma Gandhi condemned the violence and regretted the deaths of the two women, writing that Indians would not win freedom through such methods. Bal Gangadhar Tilak, in his newspaper Kesari, defended the two young men and demanded immediate swaraj, and was promptly arrested for sedition himself. The British crackdown that followed the attack led to the discovery of the Maniktala bomb factory in Calcutta and the great Alipore Conspiracy trial of 1908-09. Khudiram was tried and hanged at Muzaffarpur on 11 August 1908.

Prafulla Chaki's name is usually spoken only in the same breath as Khudiram Bose's. Yet his story stands on its own: a teenager who walked into a mission he knew might be fatal, failed to kill the man he was sent to kill, and then denied the Empire its prisoner, its trial, and its spectacle, at the cost of his own life.
