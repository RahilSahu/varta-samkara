Firoz Shah: The Mughal Prince Who Fought the British and Vanished Into Exile

Shahzada Firoz Shah was a Mughal prince who took up arms in the 1857 uprising, fought the British across Central India for nearly two years, and then did what almost no other rebel leader managed: he escaped, survived, and spent the rest of his life trying to rally foreign support for Indian independence.

He is believed to have been born in Delhi in 1832, into the Timurid house; accounts differ on his exact parentage, with some naming his father as Mirza Nizam Bakht and others describing him as a grandson of Bahadur Shah Zafar. What is better documented is his training. He was schooled in swordsmanship, horsemanship, and the arts of war, and he married Sultan Zamani Begum.

In 1857 he was returning from the Haj pilgrimage when the scale of the revolt drew him in. Still wearing the ihram of a pilgrim, he reached Mandesar in Gwalior territory on 26 August 1857 with a band of followers. A detachment of Gwalior troops stationed there, mostly Hindus from the North-Western Provinces, joined him, and the standard of revolt was raised. Moving through the region, he gathered an army reported at some 3,000 soldiers and 1,000 cavalry.

Firoz Shah was also a propagandist. The Azamgarh Proclamation of August 1857, one of the most important rebel documents of the uprising, is attributed to him. Addressed to the Hindus and Muslims of Hindustan, it called on the two communities to stay united, and promised that under a restored order all trade would be reserved for Indian merchants, all public offices would go to Indians, and the sepoys' wages would be revised upward.

His campaign then becomes a chronicle of constant movement. He occupied Dhar on 31 August 1857 and was defeated there by the Malwa Field Force on 22 October, with the fort falling on 31 October. He attacked Mehidpur on 8 November, was beaten at Mandisur on 23 November, and withdrew to Rahatgarh fort, which he left on 28 January 1858, a day before Hugh Rose captured it. He moved into Rohilkhand and left after Campbell cleared the region in May 1858. In December 1858 he operated around Etawah, where he was repulsed by A.O. Hume, the collector of Etawah, crossed the Jamuna at Uriya Ghat on 9 December, and in January 1859 joined Tantia Tope and Rao Saheb in Central India.

On 14 January 1859 the British chased Firoz Shah and Tantia Tope for 25 hours, covering 57 miles. The British set fire to the forest where Firoz Shah was hiding, but he slipped through the cordon. He was last reported at Goonah on 15 April 1859, and then he disappeared from India.

Unlike Tantia Tope, who was betrayed and hanged, Firoz Shah was never captured. He is said to have spent the years from 1857 to 1868 travelling to Afghanistan, Iran, and beyond, trying to persuade Muslim rulers to resist the British. The effort came to nothing. He briefly re-entered India in 1868 through the Swat and Buner region, then left again. He died in Mecca in 1871.

He is forgotten partly because he survived. The memory of 1857 belongs to its martyrs, and Firoz Shah left no grave in India, only a proclamation and a trail that ends in Mecca.
