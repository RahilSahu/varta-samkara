---
title: "Sangolli Rayanna: The Guerrilla Who Fought On After Kittur Fell"
date: 2026-10-02
source: "Wikipedia, NDTV"
---

Sangolli Rayanna (15 August 1798, 26 January 1831) was the military commander of Kittur who refused to accept defeat after Rani Chennamma's capture and waged what is remembered as one of India's first guerrilla wars against the British. He was betrayed, tried, and hanged from a banyan tree at the age of thirty-three.

He was born in Sangolli village of Belagavi district into a Kuruba family; his father was Bharamappa Rogannavar and his mother Kenchava. He enlisted in the army of the Kittur kingdom and rose to senior commander under Rani Chennamma. In 1824 he fought in her rebellion against the East India Company's attempt to annex Kittur. Kittur won the first battle, killing the British collector Thackeray, but lost the second; Chennamma was captured and imprisoned for life at Bailhongal, where she died in 1829. Rayanna was arrested after the defeat and released.

He did not stop. By 1829 he had resolved to install Shivalingappa, Chennamma's adopted heir, as the ruler of Kittur, and he built an insurgency out of the peasantry. His mobile bands burned British administrative offices, waylaid Company troops and plundered treasuries; he taxed the landlords to fund the revolt and was assisted by the Siddi leader Gajaveera. The British confiscated most of his land and taxed the rest punitively. They could not defeat him in open battle, so they took him by treachery: in April 1830 he was captured, along with Shivalingappa, after being betrayed. He was tried by the British and sentenced to death.

On 26 January 1831 he was hanged from a banyan tree near the village of Nandagad in Belagavi district. He was buried nearby; his associate Sangolli Bichugatti Channabasappa planted a banyan sapling on his grave, and a veeragallu memorial stone raised in the 1830s still stands on his family's ancestral land at Amatur village. In 2013 that memorial made the news when treasure hunters, set off by an unrelated gold-dig frenzy in Uttar Pradesh, uprooted the stone and dug four feet beneath it before leaving empty-handed; the police booked a case. By a coincidence his countrymen note with pride, his birth and death dates, 15 August and 26 January, later became independent India's Independence Day and Republic Day.

His name is carried by Bengaluru's main railway station, renamed Krantiveera Sangolli Rayanna station, and by schools, a museum, Kannada films and the Gee Gee ballads of North Karnataka. Yet in the national narrative he remains a footnote to the Rani's story. He was a peasant commander of a subaltern caste who kept a lost war going for two years on his own. That is not a footnote; it is the reason the Kittur rebellion is remembered as a war rather than a single defeat.
