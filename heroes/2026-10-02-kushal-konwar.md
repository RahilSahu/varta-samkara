Kushal Konwar of Assam holds a grim distinction: he was the only person in the country hanged in connection with the Quit India Movement of 1942. He was a Gandhian, a believer in truth and non-violence, and he went to the gallows for a train sabotage he had reportedly tried to prevent.

He was born on 21 March 1905 at Chaodang Chariali in the Ghiladhari Mouza of Golaghat district, then part of Sibsagar district, in Assam. As a schoolboy at Bezbaruah School he was swept up by Gandhi's non-cooperation movement in 1921. He set up a primary school at Bengmai and served as its honorary teacher, then worked for a time as a clerk at the Balijan Tea Estate. But the call of the freedom movement was stronger than the job, and he quit to devote himself to it full-time. He organized the Congress in the Sarupathar area, led satyagraha and non-cooperation campaigns, and was elected president of the Sarupathar Congress Committee.

When the Quit India Movement erupted in 1942, Assam, like the rest of India, rose in protest. Across the state, Santi Senas, peace forces, and Mrityu Bahinis, suicide squads, were formed to paralyze the British communications network, and acts of sabotage followed. The Sarupathar Congress unit devised a plan to derail a military train with the help of the local Mrityu Bahini. By one detailed account, Kushal did his best to persuade his colleagues to abstain from such violence, and failed. When the case came to court, he accepted full moral responsibility for what his movement had done.

At midnight on 10 October 1942, a train carrying British and American soldiers along with military equipment was derailed about a kilometer from Sarupathar station in Golaghat district. A large number of soldiers aboard were killed; accounts of the exact toll vary. The British cordoned off the entire region, detained people arbitrarily, and fixed on Kushal Konwar, the prominent local Congress leader, as the main conspirator. He was arrested late in 1942, reports differ on the exact date, despite the absence of conclusive evidence linking him to the act. Many advised him to go into hiding. He refused.

He was tried, convicted of conspiracy and train sabotage against the British government, and sentenced to death. At dawn on 15 June 1943, at 4:30 in the morning, Kushal Konwar was hanged in Jorhat Jail. He was thirty-eight.

Assam remembers him as Swahid, martyr. The lock-up at the Golaghat police station where he was held has been preserved as a heritage site, and chief ministers have paid tribute at his statue on his death anniversaries. Outside Assam, though, the only man hanged during Quit India remains largely unknown, a reminder of how much of the freedom struggle happened far from the cities that write its history.
