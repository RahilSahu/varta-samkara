---
title: "Kanaklata Barua: Assam's Seventeen-Year-Old Martyr"
date: 2026-10-02
source: "Vajiram and Ravi, Wikipedia"
---

On 20 September 1942, a seventeen-year-old girl led a procession to the Gohpur police station in Assam to hoist the national flag. The police warned her to turn back. She walked on, holding the flag, and they shot her dead. Her name was Kanaklata Barua, and she remains one of the youngest martyrs of India's freedom struggle.

She was born on 22 December 1924 at Borangabari village in Gohpur, then in the undivided Darrang district of Assam, to Krishna Kanta and Karneshwari Barua. Her grandfather was a noted hunter of the region, and her family traced its line to the Dolakasharia Baruas. Her mother died when Kanaklata was five; her father, who remarried, died when she was thirteen. She studied only up to class three, then left school to look after her younger siblings.

The Quit India movement of 1942 reached even remote Assam. Kanaklata joined the Mrityu Bahini, the "death squad" of young volunteers from the Gohpur subdivision who pledged to face death for independence, and despite her age she was chosen to lead its women volunteers. On 20 September the Bahini marched on the Gohpur police station to unfurl the tricolour. Kanaklata carried the flag at the head of the procession. When the police threatened to fire, she refused to stop, and she was shot dead on the spot. Another volunteer, Mukunda Kakaty, was also killed that day. The survivors carried on, and the tricolour was hoisted over the station.

Assam remembers her as Birbala and Shaheed Kanaklata, the brave girl and the martyr. The Indian Coast Guard named a fast patrol vessel ICGS Kanaklata Barua after her in 1997. Statues stand at Borngabari, at Tezpur's Kanaklata Udyan, and at Gauripur.

Assam marks 20 September in her memory every year, and her name leads the state's Martyrs' Day observances. At Tezpur, the Kanaklata Udyan rock garden carries the story of the Gohpur incident in sculpture for visitors to read as they walk. More recently, Assam's first technical university at Gohpur was named the Kanaklata Barua TVET University, a Rs 415 crore project to teach skills from AI to drone technology. It is a deliberate pairing: the state presents courage and education as the two engines of progress, and puts a seventeen-year-old martyr's name on the campus built for both.

Outside Assam she is barely known, and the reasons are plain. She died at seventeen, left no letters or speeches, and belonged to the Northeast, whose freedom struggle has always been under-covered in the national telling. Kanaklata Barua's entire political life fits inside a few months of 1942, and it ended with a single act: walking forward, flag in hand, while being told she would be shot.
