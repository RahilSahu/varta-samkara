Onake Obavva: The Woman Who Defended a Fort with a Pestle

Onake Obavva was the wife of a fort guard, and her entire recorded heroism fits into a single afternoon in 1779. Armed with nothing but an onake, the long wooden pestle used for pounding paddy, she killed the soldiers of Hyder Ali one by one as they crawled into the fort of Chitradurga through a hole in the rocks. Karnataka has celebrated her ever since, alongside Abbakka Rani and the two Chennammas, as one of its foremost women warriors.

Her husband, Kahale Mudda Hanuma, was the guard posted near a narrow opening in the rocky fortifications of Chitradurga, then ruled by the chieftain Madakari Nayaka. During Hyder Ali's final siege of the fort in 1779, his men spotted the opening and planned to send soldiers through it in single file. The guard had gone home for his lunch. When he asked for water, Obavva went to fetch it from a pond near the hole, and there she saw the enemy soldiers wriggling through.

She did not wake her husband. She picked up the pestle, hid in the darkness beside the opening, and as each soldier's head emerged she smashed it and quietly dragged the body aside, so the men behind suspected nothing. By the time her husband came looking for her, a heap of bodies lay near the passage and his wife stood there with a blood-stained onake. He sounded the bugle alarm, the fort's troops rushed out, and the attack was repelled.

She did not survive the day. Accounts differ: some say she was stabbed from behind by the invaders, others that she died of shock. The story comes down through ballads rather than official records, and the exact details, including the year of the siege, rest on that folk tradition. What is certain is the place she holds: the hole is preserved as Onake Obavvana Kindi and has become a tourist spot, a stadium in Chitradurga bears her name, and since 2021 the Karnataka government has celebrated November 11 as Onake Obavva Jayanti. The state's announcement drew a public tribute from Prime Minister Narendra Modi, who wrote that no one could forget the courage with which she fought to protect her people and culture, and hailed her as a symbol of nari shakti. The first state-sponsored celebrations were cancelled that year because the model code of conduct was in force for Legislative Council elections, but the Jayanti has been observed since. The director Puttanna Kanagal had already carried her into popular culture with a song in his 1972 classic Naagarahaavu.

Obavva belonged to the Holaya community, a detail the records preserve. She is celebrated alongside Abbakka Rani, Keladi Chennamma and Kittur Chennamma as one of Karnataka's foremost women warriors and patriots. She left no writings and commanded no armies; she simply refused to let the fort fall on her watch. The fort did fall to Hyder Ali later that year. But for one afternoon, a woman with a kitchen pestle held it.
