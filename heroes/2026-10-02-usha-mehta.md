---
title: "Usha Mehta: The Voice of the Secret Congress Radio"
date: 2026-10-02
source: "Wikipedia, Gyan Vitaranam"
---

When the British jailed the entire Congress leadership in August 1942, the freedom movement went silent on the airwaves. A twenty-two-year-old student filled the silence. Usha Mehta ran the Secret Congress Radio, an underground station that broadcast uncensored news and calls for rebellion for three months, until the British found it.

She was born on 25 March 1920 at Saras village near Surat in Gujarat. Her father was a district judge under the Raj. At five she first saw Gandhi at his Ahmedabad ashram; at eight she marched against the Simon Commission shouting "Simon Go Back." On 9 August 1942, the day the Quit India movement was to begin at Bombay's Gowalia Tank, the leaders were already arrested, and it fell to junior workers to address the crowd. Usha was among those who hoisted the tricolour that day at what is now August Kranti Maidan.

Days later, with associates including Ram Manohar Lohia, she helped set up a clandestine transmitter in Bombay. The station first went on air on 14 August 1942, opening with the words: "This is the Congress Radio calling to you from somewhere in India." It broadcast news the censored press would not carry, Gandhi's speeches, and calls for civil disobedience, shifting locations to evade detection. It stayed on air for about three months.

Among her associates in the operation was Ram Manohar Lohia. The broadcasts mixed news the British had censored with messages from underground leaders, and for a population starved of uncensored information the station became essential listening. Mehta, who went by Ushaben in those months, was its announcer: the literal voice of the Congress Radio.

The British seized the station in November 1942 and arrested Mehta. Tried for sedition, she refused to disclose the station's secrets despite threats of torture, and was sentenced to four years in prison. She was released in 1946 from Yeravda Jail. After independence she did not enter politics. She completed her doctorate on Gandhi's political and social thought, taught at Bombay University, eventually heading its civics and politics department until her retirement in 1980, and worked with the Gandhi Smarak Nidhi and the Gandhi Peace Foundation. In 1998 the Government of India conferred on her the Padma Vibhushan. She died in Mumbai on 11 August 2000, two days after attending Quit India anniversary celebrations.

Mehta is known, but dimly. The radio was a three-month episode, and she spent the next half-century as a professor rather than a politician, which the national memory rewards less. Yet in the autumn of 1942, when the movement's voice had been locked up, a young woman with a hidden transmitter was the voice it had.
