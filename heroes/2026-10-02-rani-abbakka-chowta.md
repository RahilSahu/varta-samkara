---
title: "Rani Abbakka Chowta: The Jain Queen Who Fought the Portuguese for Decades"
date: 2026-10-02
source: "The Federal, Jain Heritage Centres"
---

Rani Abbakka Chowta, the Jain queen of Ullal on the Karnataka coast, fought the Portuguese for much of the second half of the sixteenth century and refused, again and again, to pay them tribute. Her resistance predates the British Raj by more than two hundred years, which is one reason the national story of the freedom struggle has no place for her. Much of her life survives in folklore, Yakshagana theatre and folk songs rather than in dated records, so traditional accounts differ on exact years, and some scholars believe the name Abbakka may cover more than one queen of the Chowta line, merged by memory into a single legend. What follows is the account the sources agree on.

Abbakka belonged to the Chowta dynasty that ruled Tulu Nadu, and she was trained in warfare by her uncle. Though a Jain, she governed Hindus and Muslims together and raised an army drawn from all communities, with the Mogaveera fishermen and the Byari Muslims as its naval strength. The Portuguese, who had seized the Mangalore port in 1525 and wanted the spice-rich coast under tribute, found her unyielding.

The attacks came in waves. In 1555 she repelled Admiral Dom Alvaro da Silveira. In 1557 and 1558 the Portuguese plundered Mangalore, burning ships, temples and homes, but Ullal held. In 1567 they attacked Ullal directly and were resisted again. Then in 1568 General Joao Peixoto, sent by the Portuguese viceroy, captured Ullal and entered the royal court itself. Abbakka escaped to a mosque, gathered some two hundred of her soldiers that same night, and fell on the Portuguese in the dark: Peixoto was killed, seventy Portuguese soldiers were taken prisoner, and the rest retreated. In further fighting her forces killed Admiral Mascarenhas and forced the Portuguese to abandon the Mangalore fort.

In 1570 she sealed an alliance with the Bijapur Sultan and the Zamorin of Calicut, both enemies of the Portuguese; the Zamorin's general Kutty Pokar Markar destroyed the Portuguese fort at Mangalore, though he was killed on his way back. The end came through betrayal at home. Her estranged husband, who had allied with the Portuguese in exchange for land, passed on her military secrets. Captured, Abbakka is said to have revolted even in prison and died fighting.

What made her resistance possible was the state she ran. Though a Jain queen, her administration drew in Hindus and Muslims across caste lines, and her army's decisive arm was naval: the Mogaveera fishermen and the Byari Muslims of the coast, who knew every creek and current and served as her intelligence network. The Portuguese wanted the tribute she refused to pay and the spice trade she controlled; her answer was guerrilla raids on their ships, fought with intimate knowledge of the coastal terrain. During her rule, the accounts say, people of all castes and religions lived in harmony in the Ullal state.

She is honoured in coastal Karnataka with the annual Veera Rani Abbakka Utsava, statues in Ullal and Bengaluru, and a commemorative India Post stamp. Folklore credits her with the Agnivana, the fire-arrow. A queen who defied a European empire for decades deserves more than folklore, but folklore, at least, kept her alive.
