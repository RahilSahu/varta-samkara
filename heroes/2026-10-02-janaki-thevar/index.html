Janaki Thevar: The Eighteen-Year-Old Who Commanded the Rani of Jhansi Regiment in Burma

She was a teenager from a comfortable Tamil family in Kuala Lumpur who gave away her gold jewellery, defied her father, and ended up commanding an all-women regiment's Burma contingent at eighteen. Janaki Thevar, later Janaki Athi Nahappan, was the second-in-command and then commander of the Indian National Army's Rani of Jhansi Regiment, and almost nobody in India knows her name.

Janaki was born in Kuala Lumpur, in what was then British Malaya, in the mid-1920s; accounts vary between 1924 and 1926. (Her name is also spelled Janaki Devar, and occasionally rendered Davar.) She grew up in a well-to-do family and had never seen India. In July 1943, when Subhas Chandra Bose arrived in Singapore to recruit for the INA, she was in the crowd of some fifty to sixty thousand Indians who heard his speech. It changed her life. Her first act was to remove her expensive gold jewellery and donate it to the INA. Her second was to volunteer for the all-female Rani of Jhansi Regiment, formally inaugurated on 22 October 1943 on Singapore's Waterloo Street. Her father objected fiercely; after much persuasion he reluctantly signed her application, as the regiment's rules required.

One of five hundred "fighting Ranis," she trained under Captain Lakshmi Swaminathan, later Lakshmi Sahgal, through daily drills, marches and weapons training that tested a girl raised in comfort. Her determination carried her up the ranks to second-in-command. In April 1944, when Captain Lakshmi was transferred to the base hospital at Maymyo, the eighteen-year-old Janaki took over command of the regiment's Burma contingent. Her subordinates remembered her as a strict disciplinarian, firm and fair. When the British bombed the Red Cross hospital in Rangoon, she rushed in to carry the wounded out. During the INA's retreat she trekked through Burma's swamps and forests, coordinating relief for soldiers arriving at Monywa and Maymyo.

After the war and the INA's disbandment, Janaki did not fade away. She joined the Indian National Congress Medical Mission in Malaya, helped John Thivy establish the Malayan Indian Congress in 1946, and in 1949 married Athi Nahappan, the editor and publisher of the Tamil Nesan daily. She spent decades in social welfare and women's organisations, was nominated as a senator to the upper house of the Malaysian Parliament, and in 2000 received the Padma Shri from the Government of India, the first woman living outside India to be so honoured. She died of pneumonia on 9 May 2014.

Her obscurity in India is easy to explain: she lived her post-war life in Malaysia, in social work rather than politics, and the Rani of Jhansi Regiment's story is usually told through Lakshmi Sahgal alone. But for a year in Burma, an eighteen-year-old girl commanded soldiers in a war.
