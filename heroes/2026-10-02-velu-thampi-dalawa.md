---
title: "Velu Thampi Dalawa: The prime minister of Travancore who chose death over surrender"
date: 2026-10-02
source: "WION, Vajiram and Ravi, Testbook"
---

Velayudhan Chempakaraman Thampi, known to history as Velu Thampi Dalawa, was the Dalawa, or prime minister, of the princely state of Travancore from 1802 to 1809, serving under Maharaja Balarama Varma. He was born in 1765 at Thalakkulam and began his career as an ordinary taluk clerk. He rose through administrative ability and a reputation for confronting the rampant corruption of the palace and the oppressive tax collection that burdened the people. Fearing public anger, the king appointed him Dalawa, and he moved against the corrupt.

His revolt was directed at the British East India Company's interference in Travancore under the subsidiary alliance system. The British Resident, Colonel Colin Macaulay, exercised growing power over the state's affairs, and the alliance's financial demands pressed hard on Travancore. Velu Thampi, once the Company's partner in administering the state, broke with it openly.

On 11 January 1809 he issued the Kundara Proclamation at Kundara in Kollam, calling on the people of Travancore to take up arms against foreign domination. It is regarded as one of the earliest such proclamations in the history of the Indian freedom struggle, predating 1857 by nearly half a century. He organised the Travancore regular troops to attack the British force stationed at Kollam, and tens of thousands of people joined the fight.

The rebellion failed against superior British military power. The British garrison at Quilon withstood his attack, and with reinforcements the British dealt the Travancore forces a severe blow. His ally, Paliath Achan, the Dewan of Cochin, was forced to abandon the alliance and side with the British. Colonel St. Leger's forces invaded Travancore, capturing the forts of Udayagiri and Padmanabhapuram and advancing on Trivandrum. Velu Thampi fled the capital and sought refuge at the Bhagavathi Temple in Mannadi. Surrounded by British forces, he took his own life in March 1809 rather than be captured alive. The British did not stop there: his dead body was publicly hanged on a gallows on Kannammoola Hill as a warning to the people, one of the rare recorded instances of punishment after death.

The rebellion escalated quickly. His forces broke into the Cochin jail and freed all its prisoners, a marked escalation in the revolt. The British answered by landing troops under Colonel St. Leger and advancing on the capital. Velu Thampi's proclamation had called specifically on the Travancore regular troops to attack the British force stationed at Kollam, and for a time thousands answered.

He is remembered as one of the earliest pioneers of India's freedom struggle. His statue stands on the Secretariat campus in Thiruvananthapuram, a Kundara Vilambaram memorial stands at Elampallor near Kollam to mark the proclamation, and a museum dedicated to him stands at Mannadi. His double-edged sword, identified by the Kilimanoor family and handed to President Rajendra Prasad in 1957, is displayed at the Napier Museum in Kerala after 53 years at the National Museum in New Delhi. No verified contemporary portrait of him is known to survive.
