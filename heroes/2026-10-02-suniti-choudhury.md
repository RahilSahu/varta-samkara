Suniti Choudhury was born on 22 May 1917 in the Comilla sub-division of Tippera, now in Bangladesh, to Umacharan Choudhury and Surasundari Choudhury. She was a student at the Nawab Faizunnesa Girls' High School when the Civil Disobedience movement filled Bengal with picketings, processions, and arrests. Her senior, Prafulla Nalini Brahma, mentored her and supplied her with the revolutionary literature the British had banned. Vivekananda's words, that life is a sacrifice for the motherland, shaped her. She became the Major of the District Volunteer Corps and led the girls' parade when Subhas Chandra Bose came to address the student organisation. Bose, asked his view of women in revolutionary action, replied that he would be happy to see them in the front row.

On 14 December 1931, Suniti and her schoolmate Shanti Ghosh walked into the office of Charles Geoffrey Buckland Stevens, the district magistrate of Comilla, pretending to offer Christmas candies. It was Suniti who fired the first shot. Stevens died at point-blank range. She was fourteen.

The two girls were spared the death sentence on account of their age and given life imprisonment. Because she had fired first, Suniti was placed in a third-class cell alongside ordinary criminals and subjected to physical torture, which she did not yield to. In court, denied chairs, the girls stood through a nine-day hearing with their backs to the judge, smiling and singing the national anthem on their way to prison. Their only regret, as one account has it, was that their youth had denied them the gallows.

The Congress provincial government's resignation in 1939 over the non-release of political prisoners finally freed them after seven years. Suniti resumed her studies and trained as a doctor. She was offered a political career and declined it, choosing a stethoscope over a seat. She married, raised her daughter Bharati Sen, ran her clinic, and cared for her paralysed parents, earning the affectionate name "Lady Mother." She died in 1988, a year before Shanti.

In the history of India's freedom movement, Shanti and Suniti are the only two women to have successfully assassinated a British bureaucrat. Her daughter later said that Suniti never spoke of a "contribution," only that she was grateful to have had the opportunity to serve the country. The fourteen-year-old who fired the first shot grew up to spend her life healing people, and asked for nothing in return, not even remembrance. She deserves it anyway.
