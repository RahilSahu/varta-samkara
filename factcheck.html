Misleading

EAM S. Jaishankar predicted a major global food crisis specifically in 2027.

Verdict: Jaishankar warned of a major food crisis but gave no year. Speaking at the Asia Society in New York on September 28, 2026, he said there would be a major food crisis "probably in the coming months," citing the Ukraine and Iran-Gulf conflicts, Black Sea grain disruption, and fertiliser shortages. The 2027 date came from the wording of our own earlier reel headline, which we corrected in a follow-up carousel carrying his exact quote.

Why this verdict
  • The exact quote, reported by PTI, The Hindu, and The Tribune: "So there is gonna be a major food crisis probably, you know, in the coming months." No year was specified.
  • The remarks were made at an Asia Society event in New York on Monday, September 28, 2026, on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly high-level week, not at a 2027 forecast session.
  • The crisis drivers he cited were the Ukraine and Iran-Gulf conflicts disrupting grain shipments (especially through the Black Sea) and fertiliser production, which he said would strain Global South economies.
  • Our own first reel on this story (October 4, 2026) carried the headline "a major global food crisis is coming in 2027." We corrected this in a follow-up carousel the same day with the exact quote, and note the correction here.

False

The radio signal detected from Beta Pictoris b is evidence of alien technology.

Verdict: The signal is natural auroral radio emission, not a technosignature. A team led by Kevin Ortiz Ceballos at the Center for Astrophysics (Harvard and Smithsonian) used South Africa's MeerKAT array to record repeating radio bursts from the gas giant Beta Pictoris b, about 63 light-years away. The paper, posted on arXiv on September 15, 2026 and awaiting peer review, identifies the emission as auroral, produced by the same electron cyclotron maser process seen at Jupiter and Earth.

Why this verdict
  • The bursts (0.85 to 3.5 GHz) were rapid, recurring, and highly circularly polarized, matching auroral emission, not an artificial signal. The paper attributes them to magnetosphere-ionosphere coupling at the planet.
  • The finding matters because it gave the first direct measurement of an exoplanet's magnetic field strength: at least 1.25 kilogauss, more than 200 times Jupiter's field, which allows study of how planetary magnetic fields form.
  • The signal was confirmed to come from the planet, not its star, by lining up radio images against distant quasars as fixed markers. The host star is magnetically quiet, which made the detection possible.
  • The study is a preprint and had not yet completed peer review at the time of our reporting, so experts urged caution even though the natural explanation is the only one the data supports.

Misleading

The US and Australia shut their diplomatic missions in Brazil because of a confirmed security threat before the October 2026 election.

Verdict: The closures did happen, but the threat was never confirmed. On October 2, 2026, the US suspended consular services at its embassy and consulates in Brazil citing unspecified security concerns, and Australia closed its Brasilia embassy citing the US alert. Brazil's federal police carried out preventive raids in Sao Paulo and the Federal District but said investigators found no evidence corroborating the initially reported threat and identified no suspects linked to criminal organisations.

Why this verdict
  • The US State Department cited security concerns but gave no further detail on the nature of the threat or any link to the October 4 election. It did not say when services would resume.
  • Australia's ambassador, Sophie Davies, said the closure cited the US alert and advised Australians to avoid US diplomatic facilities. She did not cite an independent Australian assessment.
  • Brazil's federal police said the search-and-seizure warrants followed cooperation with foreign intelligence agencies, but no evidence backed the reported threat, and no suspects linked to criminal organisations were identified.
  • The closures came two days before the presidential first round (Lula vs Flavio Bolsonaro). The Workers' Party called them "unprecedented" and suggested political motives; the US did not link the closures to the election.

Misleading

Skeletons found at Mohenjo-daro prove that invading Aryans massacred the city's inhabitants and ended the Indus civilization.

Verdict: The skeletal evidence does not show a single massacre. Sir Mortimer Wheeler interpreted scattered skeletons at Mohenjo-daro as victims of an Aryan invasion, but the remains come from different stratigraphic levels, some from deposits that accumulated after the site was abandoned, which is inconsistent with one violent event. Archaeologist George F. Dales, who excavated at Mohenjo-daro, called the massacre account "mythical," and genome sequencing of a 4,500-year-old Rakhigarhi skeleton (Shinde et al., Cell, September 2019) found no Steppe ancestry in the Harappan population.

Why this verdict
  • Wheeler's case rested on skeleton groups at Mohenjo-daro, read as victims of invaders. For a single massacre they would need to come from the same, latest level; they do not, so they cannot be tied to one event, let alone an invasion.
  • No invader's weapons or material culture have been found at Harappan sites, while excavation shows cultural continuity, a gradual devolution of the urban tradition, not a sudden violent end.
  • The Rakhigarhi genome (one individual, published in Cell, September 2019 by Shinde and colleagues with Harvard Medical School and the Birbal Sahni Institute) indicates the Harappans were an indigenous South Asian population with ancestry traceable before 7000 BCE.
  • The study's authors argued this rejects Steppe pastoral or ancient Iranian farmers as the source of Harappan ancestry and treats the skeletons as flood victims and normal burials, not war dead. Migration remains an active academic debate, but the massacre reading of the Mohenjo-daro skeletons is not supported.
